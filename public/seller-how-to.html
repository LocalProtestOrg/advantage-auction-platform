Lesson 1

Evaluate your merchandise and research comparable sales

Before anything is numbered, decide what each item is and how it should be sold. Twenty minutes of research on the right pieces is worth more than an hour of guessing on all of them.

Sort into three groups

Research individually

Anything signed, marked, branded or made of precious metal; art, jewelry, watches, collectibles with an established market, quality furniture by a known maker, and anything you suspect is valuable.

Group candidates

Everyday household goods of modest individual value: kitchenware, linens, garden tools, books by the shelf, decor. These usually sell better as well-built lots (Lesson 2).

A third group is everything not in the sale: family keepsakes, items already promised to someone, and anything the seller cannot legally sell. Remove or close these off before you begin.

Look for marks before you look up prices

  • Turn things over. Check the underside of pottery and glass, the back of frames, the inside of drawers, clasps of jewelry, and the case backs of watches.
  • Note hallmarks and stamps such as "Sterling", "925", "14K", maker's marks, patent numbers, model numbers and paper labels. Photograph them now; you will need the pictures in the catalog.
  • Write what you find on the inventory worksheet so the research travels with the lot.

Compare with sold results, not asking prices

  • An asking price is a hope. A sold price is evidence. Search completed or sold results on marketplaces and auction result databases.
  • Compare like with like: the same maker, size, material and, above all, condition. A chip or a missing part can change the result considerably.
  • Look at several recent results and note the range, not just the highest one.
  • Use what you learn to decide how to lot the item and how to describe it, not to promise anyone a price. Auction results depend on who is bidding that week.
Pro Tip

When an item may be significant, a qualified personal property appraiser is worth the fee. Look for membership in a recognized appraisal society and training in the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP), and avoid anyone who charges a percentage of the value.1,2

Lesson 2

Build lots that encourage competitive bidding

An auction price is set by competition. A lot with one interested bidder tends to sell near its opening bid; a lot that two or more people want is where bidding happens. Good lotting is about putting interested bidders in the same contest.

★ Pro Tip

Why grouping can increase competition

Imagine three inexpensive, unbranded cat figurines: one red, one gray and one purple.

Each bidder wins her cat at the opening bid, because no one is bidding against her.

Grouped: one lot of three

To get the cat she wants, each must win the whole group, so Sarah and Rachel now bid against each other.

    Why it works: competitive bidding needs at least two interested bidders on the same lot. Sold separately, Sarah and Rachel never meet. Grouped, two buyers who wanted different things are in one contest, and the lot that nobody wanted rides along. Economic research points the same way: adding bidders tends to matter more to a seller than bargaining skill, and grouping tends to help most when interest in each separate item is thin.3,4

    This is an illustration, not a prediction. Real results depend on demand, condition, timing and how many bidders take part.

    Group when

    • Each item is modest on its own and would likely draw one bidder or none.
    • The pieces appeal to the same kind of buyer (a shelf of kitchen glass, a box of hand tools, a set of craft supplies).
    • The group can be shown clearly in one primary photograph.
    • Pickup is easier as one lot than as ten.

    Sell individually when

    • A piece has meaningful collectible value, a recognized maker, a signature or a brand with its own following.
    • It contains precious metal or gemstones, or is fine art or a quality watch.
    • It has strong independent demand: buyers search for it by name.
    • One item is worth far more than the rest. Burying it in a box lot hides it from the buyers who would compete for it.

    More lotting habits of experienced sellers

    • Keep sets complete. Six matching chairs, a dinner service or a tool set is usually worth more together. Split a set only when it is really several sets.
    • Do not mix buyer types. Fishing reels and baby clothes in one lot satisfy no one. Each lot should have an obvious buyer.
    • Count and say it. "Box lot of 24 cookbooks" sets the right expectation and is searchable.
    • Keep big and small apart. A dresser with a box of trinkets inside it complicates both pickup and description.
    • Mix your strongest lots through the catalog rather than clustering them, so bidders keep watching to the end.

    Lesson 3

    Organize the auction location efficiently

    A sale that is laid out once, in lot order, is faster to photograph, faster to catalog and much faster on pickup day.

    Set up zones

    • Work zone: folding tables in one room or garage with good light and a walkway around each table, so you can photograph from the front and both sides.
    • Photo station: one spot with a plain backdrop and steady light (Lesson 4), close to the work tables.
    • Staging zones: name each area (Table A, Garage Shelf 2, Dining Room). Record the zone for every lot on the inventory worksheet so anyone can find it later.
    • Not for sale: close rooms and cover or move anything that is not in the auction, and label it clearly.
    • Safety: anchor or lay down tall, top-heavy furniture while it is staged; tip-overs injure people.9

    Mark tables and lots with painter's tape

    • Blue painter's tape comes off wood, glass and most finishes without residue. Never put tape on gilding, paper, flaking paint, upholstery or textiles; tie on a tag or set a label on the table in front of the item instead.
    • Write the zone on tape at the corner of each table, and put a lot label on or beside each lot (see the printable labels).
    • Furniture too big for a table gets its label on the back or underside, plus a label on the floor in front of it.

    Number lots so pickup is easy

    • Number in catalog order. Lots close in catalog order, so the numbering is also the closing order.
    • Keep zones in runs: 101 to 140 for the kitchen, 141 to 180 for the garage. Nearby numbers then sit near each other on pickup day.
    • One number per lot, never reused. If a lot is withdrawn, leave its number unused rather than renumbering everything.
    • Match three places: the label on the item, the worksheet and the catalog. A mismatch is the most common pickup-day problem.
    Pro Tip

    Keep a roll of tape, a marker, the label sheets and the inventory worksheet together on one clipboard. Write each lot on the worksheet the moment it gets a number, with its zone.

    Lesson 4

    Professional merchandise photography

    The first photograph is your storefront window: it is the image buyers see in search, on the marketplace and in your catalog. Make it about the merchandise.

    The primary photo

    • The whole item (or the whole group), straight on, filling most of the frame.
    • A clean, plain background and no clutter.
    • Soft, even light; no harsh flash.
    • No prominent lot label. The primary image showcases the merchandise. The lot number is already on the listing.

    Supporting photos

    • Back, sides, top, underside and inside drawers or boxes.
    • Close-ups of signatures, maker's marks, hallmarks and model labels.
    • Every flaw: chips, cracks, stains, repairs, missing parts.
    • A photo with the lot label visible, when it helps you or the buyer match the item at pickup.
    • Something for scale, such as a coin or a tape measure, when size is not obvious.

    Light, background and camera habits

    • Light: daylight from a window or overcast outdoor light is best. Turn off overhead lights that tint the photo yellow. Avoid flash on glass, silver and framed art.6
    • Background: a plain wall, a sheet or poster board behind small items. A white or light backdrop suits most items; a darker one can suit shiny pieces.
    • Steady and level: brace your elbows or use a small tripod, tap the item to focus, wipe the lens and avoid digital zoom. Step back rather than using a wide angle up close, which bends straight edges.
    • Groups: arrange every piece so it is visible in the primary photo; the buyer should be able to count them.
    • Consistent order: shoot every lot in the same sequence (primary, sides, back, marks, flaws). Cataloging from the photos then goes quickly.
    Pro Tip

    Photograph a whole table in lot order, then catalog from the photos. Smart Catalog Tools can suggest a title, category and description from your photos for you to review and edit.

    Lesson 5

    Accurate, searchable catalog descriptions

    Buyers find lots by searching for words. A clear title brings the right bidders; an honest description keeps them happy after they win.

    A title formula that works

    What it is + maker or brand + material or style + one key detail (size, quantity or model).7

    Instead ofNice old lamp
    Box of stuff
    RARE vintage chair!!!
    WriteArt Deco Chrome Table Lamp, Frosted Glass Shade, 18 in
    Box Lot of 24 Cookbooks, Southern and Baking
    Pair of Mid-Century Walnut Armchairs, Tweed Seats

    What every description should cover

    • What it is and what is included (and anything missing: a lid, a key, a shelf).
    • Maker, marks and model exactly as they appear. If you are not sure, say "marked" or "appears to be" rather than claiming an attribution.
    • Material and period when known. Avoid "antique", "rare" or "valuable" unless you can support it.
    • Condition, specifically: "two chips on the rim, small stain on the left cushion" helps a buyer; "good condition" alone does not.
    • Dimensions and quantity: height, width and depth for furniture and art; a count for groups.
    • Pickup notes for large or fragile items (Lesson 6).
    Pro Tip

    "As is" tells buyers there is no warranty; it does not replace telling them what you know. Disclosing a flaw up front costs less than a dispute after the sale.5

    Lesson 6

    Large items and special pickup descriptions

    Tell buyers before they bid what removal will take. A buyer who arrives with the right truck and two helpers is a happy buyer; one who arrives with a sedan is a problem for everyone.

    What to check and mention

    • Stairs: how many flights, and whether turns are tight.
    • Elevators and loading docks: shared building rules, size limits and whether advance notice is needed.
    • Access restrictions: gated communities, building hours, narrow doorways or gates.
    • Parking: where a truck or trailer can stop and how far the carry is.
    • Oversized furniture: measure it and the doorways; note whether it comes apart.
    • Fragile merchandise: glass, mirrors, art and ceramics need the buyer's packing materials and padding.

    Copy-ready lot description notes

    Paste these into individual lot descriptions and edit them to fit the item. They are also in the lot editor, under the description box (choose "Add a pickup note").

    Lesson 7

    Promote your auction and choose featured merchandise

    Bidders cannot compete for lots they never see. Promotion brings more bidders into the catalog, and featured lots give them a reason to click.

    Choose featured lots

    • Pick lots with broad appeal and your best primary photographs. Featured images represent the whole sale in search, on the marketplace and in shared links.
    • Show range: one piece of furniture, one decorative or collectible piece, and one practical item reach different buyers.
    • The auction setup page shows how many featured lots your auction uses. Advantage.Bid may adjust featured selections.

    Give the auction a title people search for

    Instead ofOnline Auction
    WriteMid-Century Furniture, Tools and Vinyl Records: Westside Estate

    Spread the word

    • Share the auction link with your own email list and social media when the catalog opens, and again before closing.
    • Professional Sellers can show the auction on their own website with the Advantage.Bid widget and on their storefront page.
    • Publish the pickup details with the catalog so out-of-town bidders know what winning involves.
    • Allow enough preview time for bidders to find, watch and share lots before closing begins.

    Lesson 8

    Prepare for a smooth pickup and fulfillment

    Pickup is the last thing your buyers experience, and the thing they remember. A calm, organized pickup earns repeat bidders.

    Before pickup day

    • Stage sold lots by lot number or by buyer, separate from anything not sold.
    • Send access instructions: entrance, parking, stairs and what to bring.
    • If you use appointments, keep them within the published pickup window.
    • Prepare large and fragile lots: clear the path, protect floors and door frames.

    At the door

    • Check the buyer's identity and paid invoice on Advantage.Bid before anything leaves.
    • Mark each lot released on the pickup list as it goes out.
    • Write down anything unusual: a missing piece, a damaged item, a lot left behind.
    • Keep the pickup area separate from rooms that are not part of the sale.

    Uncollected merchandise and exceptions

    Follow the pickup terms published with the auction and the platform's procedures for missed pickups. Keep a short written note of what happened and of your messages with the buyer. The published pickup date and window are part of the terms; you may choose to accommodate a buyer, which does not change the terms.

    Safety

    • Work with at least one other person, keep cash and valuables out of sight, and lock rooms that are not part of the sale.
    • Let buyers do the heavy lifting with their own helpers; lifting and carrying heavy loads is a leading cause of injury.10
    • Payment happens on Advantage.Bid. Never accept a check or a transfer for "the balance" or an overpayment; those are common scams.8
