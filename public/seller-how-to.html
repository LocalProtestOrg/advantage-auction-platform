Organize the sale with tables and painter's tape

A sale that is laid out once, in lot order, is faster to photograph, faster to catalog and much faster on pickup day.

1. Clear a work area

Set up folding tables in one room or garage with good light. Leave a walkway around each table so you can photograph from the front and both sides.

2. Group before you number

Put like with like: kitchen, linens, tools, books, decor, art, furniture. Decide what sells as one lot (a set of six chairs) and what sells alone (a signed print). Small items of little value sell better grouped into a box lot.

3. Mark tables and lots with painter's tape

  • Use blue painter's tape: it comes off wood, glass and finishes without residue. Never put tape on gilding, paper, flaking paint, upholstery or textiles; use a tag tied with string or a label on the table in front of the item instead.
  • Write a table number on tape at the corner of each table (Table A, Table B).
  • Give each lot a tape label with its lot number, or use the printable lot labels below. Put the label where it shows in the photo but does not cover a mark or a flaw.
  • Furniture too big for a table gets its tape label on the back or underside, plus a label on the floor in front of it.
Tip: keep a roll of tape, a marker and the inventory worksheet together on a clipboard. Write each lot on the worksheet the moment it gets a number.

Lot numbering

  • Number in the order buyers will see them. Lots close one minute apart, so the order is the closing order. Start strong: put a few of your best lots early to draw bidders in, and spread your three featured lots through the sale.
  • Keep groups together. Number a room or category in one run (101 to 140 for the kitchen, 141 to 180 for tools). Pickup is easier when nearby numbers sit near each other.
  • One number per lot, never reused. If a lot is withdrawn, leave its number unused rather than renumbering everything.
  • Match three places: the label on the item, the worksheet and the catalog. A mismatch is the most common pickup-day problem.

Photography that sells

  • Light: daylight from a window or overcast outdoor light is best. Turn off overhead lights that cast yellow or mixed color. No flash on glass, silver or framed art.
  • Background: a plain wall, a sheet or a sheet of poster board behind small items. Move clutter out of the frame.
  • First photo: the whole item, straight on, filling the frame, with its lot label visible. This is the photo buyers see in search.
  • Then: both sides, the back, the top, the inside of drawers, and close-ups of signatures, maker's marks, labels and hallmarks.
  • Show flaws honestly: chips, cracks, stains, repairs and missing parts each get a clear close-up. Honest photos prevent disputes and returns.
  • Scale: for items where size is not obvious, include a common object or a tape measure in one photo, and enter dimensions in the catalog.
  • Hold the phone level and step back rather than using a wide angle up close, which bends straight edges.
Tip: photograph a whole table in lot order, then catalog from the photos. Smart Catalog Tools can suggest a description from your photos for you to review and edit.

Pickup preparation

  • Stage by lot number after the auction closes, so each buyer's lots can be found in minutes. Keep sold lots together and separate from anything not in the sale.
  • Set up a pickup table at the door with the pickup list, a pen, tape and bags or boxes for small lots.
  • Release only paid lots: check the buyer's name and paid invoice on Advantage.Bid before anything leaves. The buyer's full pickup address details are shared only after payment.
  • Large items: tell buyers in advance if they need helpers, a truck or tools. Clear a path to the door before pickup starts.
  • Keep to the published pickup window. It is part of the terms buyers agreed to.
  • Safety: work with at least one other person, keep cash and valuables out of sight, and keep rooms that are not part of the sale closed.
